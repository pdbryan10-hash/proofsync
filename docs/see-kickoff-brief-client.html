Kick-off: joining Concerto and Joblogic

SEE · ProofSync 90 minutes proof-works.co.uk

This session is where the work gets specified. Most of it is spent watching your coordinator move one job each way between the two systems, because everything we build is driven by what is actually on those screens — and none of that exists in any documentation.

01

What is being joined up

  1. 01 A job is raised by your client in Concerto, and your desk picks it up. in scope
  2. 02 The same job is entered in Joblogic so it can be planned and worked. in scope
  3. 03 The engineer attends and completes it in Joblogic. unchanged
  4. 04 The completion goes back into Concerto, with the certificates and job sheets. in scope

Step three does not change: your engineers carry on exactly as they do now. Steps one, two and four are where the same job details get typed into a second screen.

The session is built around two live examples — one job coming in, and one job going back — worked on screen by whoever does it every day.

With your agreement we record the screen share and capture the two forms. The recording shows the path; the capture gives the field names, dropdown values and validation messages that make it buildable. Nothing needs preparing beyond having a live example of each to hand.

02

A word for whoever is sharing their screen

This is not a study of how fast anyone works, and nobody is being assessed. We record the screens rather than the person, and what we take off the recording is field names, dropdown values and the exact wording of validation messages. Going at your normal pace, including the bits that are fiddly, is more useful to us than a tidy run.

What the software takes on is the typing — the same job details keyed a second and a third time into a second and a third screen. What it does not take on is the judgement: knowing a priority is wrong for that site, spotting that the asset does not match, knowing which client wants what and which contractor needs chasing. That stays exactly where it is.

It also proposes rather than acts. Jobs are put forward for a person to accept, writes are approved before they are submitted, and anything ambiguous is handed back rather than guessed — so the desk keeps the final say on every job. The person best placed to give that say-so is the person who does the work today, which is why the map we build in this session is largely theirs.

05

The ground each block covers

How a job reaches your desk

Whether it arrives by email out of Concerto, in a queue somebody opens, or another way entirely; who watches it and in what hours; what happens overnight and at weekends. Also whether there is an acknowledge or accept step with a clock on it, how a change made by the client after raising shows up, and whether jobs get cancelled or arrive twice.

One job coming in

Watched once at normal speed, then walked through slowly. What is copied and what is looked up; how a Concerto priority becomes a Joblogic priority and its dates; whether Concerto's sites and assets line up with Joblogic's or need translating; whether anything the client attaches has to reach the engineer. Where the coordinator judges rather than copies, we want to hear it said out loud — it either becomes a rule worth writing down, or it stays a decision a person makes.

Where the Concerto reference is recorded on the Joblogic job is the single most important detail in the session. It is the key every later step matches on, so we will want to see the field it goes in — and what happens on the rare occasion it is entered wrongly.

One job going back

The completion form field by field: what is mandatory, what validation fires and in what words, the formats for dates, durations and time on site, the dropdown values your desk actually uses, and anything that must never be touched. Then whether completing changes status or closes the job, and who is permitted to do that.

And the read-back: what your coordinator looks at to be satisfied it landed. That view is what every write gets compared against.

Certificates and job sheets

Which documents have to reach Concerto and where each comes from — generated by Joblogic, produced by the engineer on site, uploaded by the office, or issued by a third party days later. Late-arriving certificates matter more than they sound: they decide whether a job is written back once or twice. We will also want the upload rules — accepted types, size limits, whether a category has to be chosen, any naming convention your client expects, and anything that must be signed first.

Access

The operator signs in as one of your users, so the account is yours: visible in your directory and revocable by you. That means an email account on your domain, a named login to each system — the same access your coordinator already has, not a new grant from your client — the MFA method on both and who holds the second factor, and written confirmation from SEE that this use is authorised. Everything starts read-only.

Two minutes we would like to borrow on the call: sign the same Concerto login in twice from two machines. If the second session evicts the first or locks the record, the operator needs its own account rather than sharing your coordinator's — cheaper to know now than mid-run.

Scope and exceptions

Which sites come first, which job types are in and which are deliberately out, volumes each way and the hours they land in. Then the cases that do not fit — no reference, an ambiguous match, a site that does not exist in the other system, a rejected form — and who at SEE picks those up.

07

How we work in your systems

Nothing is written to prove that writing works. The route is proven offline first. Live writes exist only to do real work.
No test record is created in your live system, and none will be asked for.
MFA is satisfied, never bypassed. A person signs in each day and starts the run; we never hold a password.
A missing reference or an ambiguous match becomes an exception. It is never a guess.
Every write is read back and compared before it counts as done.
08

What you have at the end

  • A screen and field map of both systems in both directions — documentation of your own process that does not currently exist anywhere, and yours to keep
  • How long the transfer itself takes each way, taken from the recording, set against your daily volumes — a measure of the round trip, not of anyone working it
  • A phase plan with dates: read-only proof, offline build, then the first attended write
09

What happens next

The build runs against the map rather than against your systems. The whole journey is proven offline first, both directions, including the paths that fail: no match, an ambiguous match, a site that does not exist in the other system, a rejected form, a session that expires mid-job. You see that run and agree it is right before anything is pointed at live.

The first live runs are on real jobs, with someone from your team present, and each write approved before it is submitted. That approval step relaxes only when you have watched enough of them land correctly and say so. The daily sign-in stays attended.
